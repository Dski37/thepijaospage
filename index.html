The Pijao People

Guardians of the Magdalena River valley and the Central Andes mountains. A resilient indigenous community that fought historical battles for nearly a century and continues to defend their land, identity, and oral memory in Tolima, Colombia.

Origins and Identity

Who Are the Pijao People?

The Pijao people, historically also known as Coyaimas and Natagaimas, are an emblem of indigenous resilience in western-central Colombia. They mainly inhabit the department of Tolima, specially in municipalities such as Coyaima, Natagaima, Ortega, Chaparral, San Antonio, and San Antonio de Calarcá. Smaller communities also live near the neighboring departments of Huila and Cauca.

Before the Spanish conquest, the Pijaos were not a single centralized empire. Instead, they formed a powerful confederation of independent native communities. They shared a common cultural identity, trade networks, and a deep respect for freedom. They used to live along the fertile valleys of the Magdalena, Saldaña, and Coello rivers, where they practiced farming, hunting, and fishing.

Spanish chroniclers described the Pijao warriors as one of the bravest and most courageous groups in the Americas. Today, thousands of families proudly embrace their Pijao heritage, keeping their ancestral memory alive through communal work and traditional assemblies.

“Being Pijao today means honoring our ancestors, defending our rivers, and protecting our mother earth with pride and unity.”

Geographical Homeland

Ancestral Territory

The ancestral world of the Pijao spans from the high peaks of the Central Andes cordillera down to the warm valley of the Magdalena River. The rivers in this region—such as the Saldaña, Coello, and Magdalena—have always been essential for their survival, serving as fishing spots, transport routes, and sacred spaces.

Today, most Pijao indigenous reservations (Resguardos) are located in the southern municipalities of Tolima. You can feel their vibrant culture in local traditions, traditional foods, folklore, and community farming.

Historical Legacy

History and Indigenous Resistance

Before 1500

Life Before the Conquest

Pijao ancestors used to live freely across the Andes and river valleys. They cultivated corn, cassava (yucca), beans, squash, and cotton. They were skilled potters and goldsmiths, trading handcrafted goods with neighboring tribes.

16th Century (1500s)

Arrival of Spanish Conquistadors

When the Spanish colonizers arrived, the Pijaos organized a fierce military defense of their territory. Unlike other groups, they refused to surrender, delaying colonial expansion between Santafé (Bogotá), Popayán, and Quito for decades.

1600 – 1615

The Pijao Wars & Cacique Calarcá

Under legendary leaders like Cacique Calarcá and Cacique Laarcaguala, the Pijao confederation fought courageous battles. Although diseases and war reduced their population, their resistance became legendary in South American history.

17th – 19th Century

Survival and Resguardos

During the colonial era, the Spanish crown created specific reservation areas like Coyaima and Natagaima. Even though they lost their fluent native language over time, they preserved their farming habits, spiritual myths, and natural medicine.

20th Century – Today

Land Rights and Reorganization

In recent decades, Pijao communities organized local councils (Cabildos) to reclaim their ancestral lands. Colombia's 1991 Constitution officially recognized indigenous autonomy, empowering Pijao governance today.

Traditions & Spirituality

Culture, Worldview, and Traditions

Mother Earth (Lulomoy)

Spiritual life centers on deep respect for nature. They revere Mother Earth, known spiritually as Lulomoy. Rivers, mountains, and forests are sacred beings that must be protected and respected.

Sacred Corn & Gastronomy

Corn is the foundation of life and community. From corn, they prepare traditional Chicha (fermented beverage used in rituals), Tamales Tolimenses, and Lechón. They also cook river fish like Viudo de Pescado.

El Mohán & Guardians

Traditional folklore includes protective spirits like El Mohán, the guardian of rivers and waters. Wise elders and traditional healers (Curanderos) hold essential knowledge about medicinal plants.

Music & Celebrations

Their musical identity includes regional rhythms like Bambuco, Sanjuanero, and Rajaleña. They play cane flutes (flautas de carrizo), drums, maracas, and the traditional ciempiés instrument during San Juan festivals in June.

Body Painting & Craftsmanship

Historically, Pijao warriors painted their bodies with natural dyes from achiote (bija) and jagua. Today, artisan pottery and weaving preserve these ancient geometric patterns.

The Minga System

Community life relies on the Minga—a collective work tradition where community members collaborate to build roads, harvest crops, or organize events for the shared good without monetary pay.

Language & Local Slang

Understanding Pijao Words: The Term «Ta»

Although the original Pijao language became extinct in its fluent form during the 20th century, the community has preserved valuable native vocabulary inside the Spanish spoken in Tolima.

How to use the slang word «Ta» or «Tane»:

The word «Ta» is an honorific term used to express affection, respect, and reverence toward elders, grandparents, or wise community leaders.

Example greeting: "¿Cómo está, Ta?" (How are you, Elder / Teacher?)
Learning tip: Simply add "Ta" at the end of a simple greeting when addressing an elder as a sign of traditional respect!

Current Context

The Pijao Community Today

The Pijao people are very active today. Thousands of individuals identify as Pijao and organize into local Cabildos and indigenous reserves across southern Tolima. Their leaders represent the community before state institutions to defend their rights.

Their main struggles center on protecting water sources against environmental threats, recovering stolen lands, promoting intercultural healthcare, and teaching Pijao history in local schools.

  • Land Titling: Reclaiming and legalizing ancestral territory for indigenous reserves (Resguardos).
  • Indigenous Education: Schools that teach Pijao history, environmental ethics, and traditional crafts.
  • Traditional Medicine: Preserving healing knowledge and native plant remedies with community healers.
  • Indigenous Guard: Unarmed community guardians who peacefully protect territory and local peace.
  • Water Defense: Protecting rivers like Saldaña and Coello from industrial pollution and mining projects.
